# Mast-base button pusher

## What it is

A small plastic clip that lives on a windsurf mast extension. You push it and it
presses the mast base's spring-loaded release button for you, so you can get the
base out without jamming a thumb into a stiff metal button.

## How it should work

The clip is a curved shell that wraps most of the way around the extension tube,
more than half, so it can't fall off sideways. It doesn't touch the tube
everywhere. It only touches in three places:

- Two bumps on the inside at the back, described below.
- The two ends of the shell, which hug the tube and hold everything in place.

Everywhere in between, the shell sits slightly off the tube with a small air gap.

To use it, you sit the clip on the tube and locate it so the small bump drops
into the hole. That fixes its position. Then you put a thumb on the outside of
the shell, directly behind the big bump, and push straight in toward the tube.
The whole clip moves in a little, the two ends ride outwards on the tube, the big
bump goes down and the button releases. Take your thumb off and it springs back
out.

It's a straight push with one thumb. Nothing gets squeezed, pinched or levered.

There is no hinge, no thin flexing tongue, no window cut through the wall. The
whole body flexes a bit, all over. That's the point. It's what makes it last.

## The two bumps

**The pusher.** The bigger of the two. Rounded, with a shallow dish in the tip so
it cups the round metal button instead of sliding off the side of it. It should
reach in far enough to push the button all the way down, plus a bit of margin,
and no further.

**The locator.** Smaller, and it drops into the hole that sits close to the
button. This is what stops the clip drifting or turning, so the pusher stays
where it belongs and the user doesn't have to hold alignment while pushing. It's
the difference between a clip you have to fiddle with and one that just clicks
into place.

The locator has three rules. It must be a comfortable bit narrower than the hole,
so it goes in first time with cold, wet, sandy hands. It must be tapered and
rounded so that when the clip is pushed in, it simply slides deeper into the hole
rather than jamming, binding or shoving the clip off to one side. And it must be
shallow enough that a firm pull lifts it back out when you want to move or remove
the clip. If it grips like a peg, the clip becomes a nuisance.

The locator is for position only. It should never be what's holding the clip on
the tube or taking the load when the button pushes back. The three contact points
do that.

## Bump spacing

The distance between the two bumps is a number someone will need to set to match
their own gear.

Measure it as the clear gap between the two bumps, not centre to centre. In other
words, the distance from the near edge of one bump to the near edge of the other,
following the curve of the tube's surface rather than straight through the air.
This is the same thing you'd measure on the extension itself: the bare stretch of
metal between the edge of the button and the edge of the hole.

The reason for doing it this way is that it's what someone can actually measure
with a ruler on their own gear, and it stays meaningful if either bump changes
size. If the pusher gets bigger, the gap stays what it was and the bumps just
move apart on their own.

Both bumps and both features on the extension sit on one line, so whoever builds
this should place the pair along that line and keep the gap as a single
adjustable number.

## Supporting the bumps

Shell height is its own setting and shouldn't move on its own. Someone picks a
height because that's what feels right in the hand and looks right on the gear,
and it should stay where they put it.

That means the bumps can end up sitting near, or past, the edge of a short shell.
That can't be left as a bump hanging off a thin rim.

Wherever a bump lands close to an edge, the shell should grow a local swelling
around it, blended smoothly into the surrounding wall, so the bump is always
sitting on solid material with a generous fillet at its base. Think of a gentle
bulge in the shell's edge that reaches out just far enough to cradle the bump,
not a bolted-on boss and not a sharp collar. If a bump falls entirely outside the
shell, the swelling becomes a small rounded ear that carries it, still smoothly
blended and still self-supporting.

This should happen by itself when the numbers change. Someone should be able to
set a short shell with widely spaced bumps and get a sound part without noticing
there was a problem to solve.

Both bumps need the same clear air behind them, so the shell has to stand off the
tube far enough that both can travel when the clip is pushed in.

## The ends, and why they're lopsided

The two ends flare outwards into lips, like the mouth of a funnel. This lets you
push the clip onto the tube by hand without fighting it.

The ends also need to reach far enough round the tube, comfortably past the
halfway point, that when the button pushes back the clip can't simply slide off.
It has to be geometry holding it on, not friction.

There's a second job the ends have to do, and it's about how the clip sits when
nobody is touching it, not about what happens during the push. Once a thumb is on
it the user's force swamps anything the clip does on its own, so the moment of
the push isn't the concern.

The concern is the clip just resting on the tube. If the pusher ends up out on an
ear at one edge, the three points it stands on are no longer spread around it.
Two of them sit up at the shell's height and the third is out on its own past the
edge, so the clip is standing on a lopsided tripod. It rocks. It sags toward the
loaded side, doesn't sit square on the tube, and the pusher no longer points
properly at the button, which then makes it fiddly to seat and easy to knock out
of place while carrying the rig.

The fix is for the ends to grow taller on that same side, so their grip on the
tube reaches out level with the pusher. The ends stop being short patches of
contact and become longer strips running along the tube, spanning far enough that
there is always material bearing on the tube alongside wherever the pusher sits.
The clip then stands on a wide, balanced base and sits square by itself.

So the ends are deliberately asymmetric. They extend further on whichever side
the pusher has drifted to and stay at the shell's normal height on the other. The
extension should be a smooth flowing continuation of the shell's edge, tapering
back to normal as it goes round toward the back, not a bolted-on tab. If the
pusher moves back inside the shell as the numbers change, the ends should relax
back to symmetric on their own.

Keep the flare gentle and rounded, not a sharp hook. Nothing on this part should
be able to catch a finger, a sail, or a line.

## The back

The outside of the shell directly behind the pusher is the thumb pad. It wants to
be broad and slightly dished so a thumb sits in it and doesn't slide off to one
side.

## Shape notes

The curve is not a circle and probably not a true ellipse either. What matters is
the behaviour, not the name of the curve:

- Tightest at the two ends, where it grips.
- Roomier around the back, so the bumps have somewhere to travel and the tube has
  somewhere to go when the clip is pushed in.
- The transition between the two should be smooth and gradual. The shell should
  look like it's hugging the tube naturally, with no discrete pads, feet or steps
  on the inside face. If someone can point at a spot and say "that's the contact
  pad," it's wrong. The contact should just be where the curve happens to come
  closest.

Whatever curve gets used should be defined by a handful of meaningful numbers,
not by hand-placed control points, so it can be re-tuned later.

## Push feel

This is the thing most likely to need a couple of rounds of tuning. It has to be
soft enough that a cold, wet hand can push it one-handed, and stiff enough that
it doesn't feel floppy. Wall thickness is the obvious dial. Expect to print two
or three thicknesses and pick by feel.

Bear in mind the person is pushing against two things at once: the button's own
spring, and the clip's resistance to being pushed in. Keep the clip's share of
that well under the button's, or it'll feel dead.

Shell height affects this too, and so do the taller ends. Both make the clip
stiffer. Settle the height and the bump positions first, then adjust wall
thickness to suit.

## What needs to be adjustable

Different brands of extension differ, so someone should be able to change these
without redrawing anything:

- Tube diameter.
- Where the button sits, height-wise.
- Button size, and how far the pusher reaches in.
- Locator size, and how far it reaches in.
- Clear gap between the two bumps.
- How much air gap there is between shell and tube.
- Wall thickness.
- How far round the tube the shell wraps.
- Shell height, set independently.
- How much the ends flare.

Sensible starting point: a tube about 48 mm across, button centre about 13 mm up
from the bottom edge of the clip, button about 8 mm across, pusher reaching in
about 3 mm, locator noticeably smaller and reaching in a little less, air gap
around 3-4 mm, walls around 2.5-3 mm, wrapping around 250 degrees, shell around
25 mm tall. Bump gap to be measured off real gear.

## Making it

Meant to be 3D printed, standing upright, no support material. Nothing should
overhang badly or need bridging, including the swellings around the bumps and the
taller ends. Everything that can be rounded off should be.

Print it in something tough and springy that doesn't mind sun, salt and being
left in a hot car. PLA is not suitable. It should tolerate living on the gear
permanently, so avoid making it grip the tube so tightly that the plastic slowly
relaxes and loses its hold over a season.

## Also worth producing

A quick test piece, a short slice of the same shell carrying both bumps, so
someone can check the fit, the gap and whether the locator finds its hole, in a
two-minute print before committing to the real thing.

## Things to check on a physical sample

- It clips on and off by hand, wet, without tools.
- Resting on the tube untouched, it sits square and doesn't rock or sag to one
  side.
- The locator finds its hole easily and gives a clear seated feel.
- Once seated, the clip can't turn or slide on its own, and stays put while the
  rig is carried around.
- A single thumb push releases the base.
- The locator slides deeper as you push and never binds.
- A firm pull lifts it back out without a struggle.
- It springs fully back every time.
- It survives a few hundred pushes without cracking, especially around the bump
  supports, any ears, and where the taller ends blend back into the shell.
- Nothing sharp anywhere.
